The idea

Physical retail has a practice called walking the store. A leader comes in through the front door like a customer, follows the signage, picks up products, stands in line, and writes down everything that made buying harder. I ran the digital version of this play for years — site merchandising and storefront management at Amazon, mobile game distribution across every North American storefront before that. The mechanics translate directly.

The problem it solves: product owners are often too close to their own product. You know where everything is. You wrote the copy and you can't unread it. The fastest way out of your own head is to become someone else — a specific, qualified customer with a reason to be shopping today — and walk every path they'd walk.

The skill builds that persona first, then shops each channel where buyers actually meet you: Google results, your homepage, your pricing page, the Amazon search grid, the App Store listing. It shops in two modes — lean back (skimming on the couch, three seconds per screen) and lean forward (comparison shopping, doing the price math, reading your worst review). Then it writes up what it found.

What you get

A walk report where every finding is a work order: where it is, what the shopper saw, why it matters, the exact fix with replacement copy written out, numbered steps on the tools you actually use, and how to check it worked. Screenshots ride along as numbered exhibits, embedded where they're discussed. A sample finding:

Shipping cost first appears at the payment step — sales

Where: checkout, step 3 of 3, after email + address entry
Saw: $8.95 shipping surfaces only at payment; the product page and cart never mention it
Matters: this buyer did their budget math on the product page — a surprise cost at the last step is the classic abandonment trigger
Fix: state the shipping policy on the product page and in the cart
Effort: hours

Verbatim first impressions stay in the report ("I can't find the price"), because that's the evidence. Each report ends with a measurement plan: a baseline captured during the walk, the metric each fix should move, a tag for your analytics — and it schedules its own re-walk, 60–90 days out, so before and after sit side by side.

Run a walk

Paste a prompt, name your store, and describe your buyer in plain words — the skill turns that into a full persona. If you have Claude in Chrome, Claude shops your store live and captures the screenshots itself.

Walk the store for [myshop.com]. The buyer is someone shopping for their first vitamin C serum, comparing brands on Instagram and Google.
Walk my Amazon listing [URL]. Compare it against page 1 for the search term "[what your buyer types]".

  • Name the buyer if you know them; the skill builds the persona either way, but you know things it doesn't.
  • Let it walk channels you didn't ask about. The gap between your site and your listing is usually a top finding.
  • Run it before a launch, after a redesign, and any time you catch yourself saying "customers just don't get it."

# Walk the Store — free Claude skill (MIT)

Audits a digital storefront — a DTC or SaaS website, a marketplace listing, an app store page — by shopping it as a specific, qualified customer persona. Output: a walk report with findings ordered by impact, written as work orders (Where / Saw / Matters / Fix / Steps / Verify / Effort), numbered screenshot exhibits embedded inline, and a measurement plan (walk tag `wts-YYYY-MM-DD`, external baseline, re-walk at 60-90 days).

Author: Jeremy Peronto — https://jeremyperonto.com

Store owners (Shopify / Etsy / own site), marketplace sellers (Amazon / Walmart / TikTok Shop), app founders (listing + site consistency), agencies running repeatable evidence-backed audits.

## Method (short)

1. Scope: product, every channel the buyer crosses, the owner's stack, where the report will be read.
2. Persona: real enough to shop like one person, broad enough to stand for a segment, aligned with who the product is priced for.
3. Walk each channel twice: lean-back (3-second skim) and lean-forward (comparison shopping, price math, worst review).
4. Cross-channel consistency check (price, claims, story).
5. Findings as work orders, impact order, exhibits embedded.
6. Baseline + walk tag + scheduled re-walk.
